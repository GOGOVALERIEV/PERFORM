The Cold War, a period of geopolitical tension between the United States and the Soviet Union, began shortly after the end of World War II. In 1947, the Truman Doctrine was announced, outlining the U.S. policy of containment of communism. This policy aimed to prevent the spread of Soviet influence and was a significant step in the escalating rivalry. The same year, the Marshall Plan was implemented, providing approximately 13 billion dollars in aid to Western Europe. This economic assistance was intended to rebuild war-torn nations and counter the appeal of communism.

Military alliances further solidified the divide between the two superpowers. In 1949, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) was founded, creating a collective defense pact among Western nations. In response, the Soviet Union established the Warsaw Pact in 1955, uniting the Eastern Bloc countries under a similar alliance. These alliances were a clear indication of the bipolar world order that had emerged, with the world largely divided between those aligned with the United States and those aligned with the Soviet Union.

The construction of the Berlin Wall in 1961 exemplified the deepening of this divide. Overnight, the wall was erected to separate East and West Berlin, symbolizing the Iron Curtain that had descended across Europe. This physical barrier was a stark reminder of the ideological and political differences that characterized the Cold War era. It was during this period that the world came closest to nuclear conflict, during the Cuban Missile Crisis in October 1962. The crisis, which lasted for 13 days, was a tense standoff between the U.S. and the Soviet Union over the placement of Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba.

In the 1970s, a period known as détente saw a temporary easing of tensions. This period was marked by arms control treaties, such as the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT), which aimed to limit the nuclear arsenals of both superpowers. As Winston Churchill noted in 1946, "An iron curtain has descended across the continent," but by the 1970s, there were signs of a thaw in relations.

The late 1980s brought significant changes that would ultimately lead to the end of the Cold War. In 1985, Soviet leader Gorbachev introduced policies of glasnost and perestroika, which promoted openness and restructuring within the Soviet Union. These reforms had a profound impact on the Eastern Bloc, leading to increased political freedoms and economic changes. On November 9, 1989, the Berlin Wall fell, marking a symbolic end to the division of Europe. This historic event was followed by the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991, effectively ending the bipolarity that had defined the Cold War era.
