The Cold War, a period of intense geopolitical tension between the United States and the Soviet Union, began shortly after the end of World War II, reshaping the global landscape forever. Initially, the Truman Doctrine of 1947 outlined U.S. containment policy to halt the spread of communism. Military alliances deepened the divide. NATO's foundation in 1949 created a Western defense pact, while the Soviet Union responded with the Warsaw Pact in 1955. Clearly, a bipolar world order had emerged, solidifying alignments around the two superpowers.

A stark symbol of this division was the Berlin Wall, erected overnight in 1961. It forcibly separated East and West Berlin, embodying the Iron Curtain's ideological differences. The Cold War's most perilous moment came during the Cuban Missile Crisis in October 1962. For 13 tense days, the U.S. and Soviet Union faced off over Soviet missiles in Cuba, bringing the world dangerously close to nuclear conflict.

By the late 1980s, significant changes led to the Cold War's end. A crowning moment came on November 9, 1989, when the Berlin Wall fell, symbolizing Europe's reunification. The Soviet Union's dissolution in December 1991 marked the definitive end of the Cold War's bipolar world, transforming global politics once again.
